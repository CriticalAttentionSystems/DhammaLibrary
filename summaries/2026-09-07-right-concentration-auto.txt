Concentration within the whole path

Bhante G presents right concentration as the culmination of the Noble Eightfold Path, supported by all its other factors. It cannot be separated from understanding, intention, moral conduct, effort, and mindfulness. Liberation is not a matter of waiting for a fortunate accident while neglecting that training.

He explains right concentration through the four jhānas, or meditative absorptions. Each has conditions that must be developed, and each becomes familiar through repeated practice.

Seclusion and the five hindrances

Physical seclusion means finding a place with fewer disturbances and less human activity. Bhante G does not restrict this possibility to monastics. Mental seclusion requires freedom from the hindrances that scatter and burden the mind:

- Sensual desire is compared to being in debt.
- Ill will is compared to sickness.
- Sleepiness and drowsiness are compared to imprisonment.
- Restlessness and worry are compared to slavery.
- Doubt is compared to being lost in a desert without clear direction.

Overcoming each brings a corresponding sense of relief and gladness. The comparison makes clear why concentration is more than forcing attention onto an object while leaving the mind's conflicts untouched.

The first jhāna

Along with overcoming the hindrances, cultivate wholesome thought: letting go, loving-friendliness, and compassion. Bhante G explains directed and sustained thought in relation to these wholesome qualities, including the continued support and repetition of them.

Joy and happiness develop from seclusion. This happiness is not the excitement of winning, succeeding, or acquiring something. It calms and relaxes the mind and body, making collectedness possible. The bath-powder simile illustrates how thoroughly the whole body can be pervaded by the experience.

At this stage the hindrances are still close enough to intrude. Mindfulness is needed to recognize and protect the conditions supporting the attainment.

Familiarity and mastery

A pleasant experience alone is not mastery. Bhante G repeatedly asks practitioners to remember the steps by which the state was reached, enter it deliberately, determine a period for remaining, emerge, and review the process. Repetition makes its conditions clearer and allows the mind to move toward a subtler state.

This emphasis also prevents the experience from becoming merely a cherished memory. The practitioner needs to understand how it arose and how it can be developed again.

The second, third, and fourth jhānas

In the second jhāna, directed and sustained thought subside. Inner confidence, clarity, and unification of the mind become prominent; joy and happiness arise from concentration. Bhante G describes a deeper mental silence, beyond simply refraining from outward speech. He connects it with learning to attend without continual verbalizing.

In the third jhāna, the mind moves beyond the earlier joy toward equanimity, while a quieter happiness remains. It becomes less fascinated by the excitement of a newly attained state and more balanced.

In the fourth jhāna, pleasant and unpleasant feeling are transcended, and mindfulness and equanimity mature together. Bhante G presents this as a particularly clear, steady foundation for further development. His illustrations of water, a pond, and pervasion of the body convey the collected and cooling character of these states.

Directing concentration toward liberation

At the refined level of concentration, the mind may continue developing meditative attainments or be directed toward insight and the destruction of defilements. Bhante G refers to the Buddha's recollection of past lives and knowledge of the passing and rebirth of beings, distinguishing such attainments from the decisive ending of the defilements.

The purpose of the path is freedom from suffering. Right concentration is powerful because the whole training supports it, and because that collected mind can be used for liberating understanding.
