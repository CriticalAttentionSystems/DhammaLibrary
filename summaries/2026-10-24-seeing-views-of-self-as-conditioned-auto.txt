A quarrel and a question

Bhante G turns from the preceding series on the establishments of mindfulness to the Pārileyyaka Sutta (SN 22.81). The discourse opens at Kosambi, where the Buddha puts his lodging in order and leaves alone without informing his attendant or the community. Bhante G supplies background about a dispute among monks that began over water left in a washing vessel. As he tells it, a minor breach grew into a quarrel, and the monks refused the Buddha’s advice to live harmoniously. The Buddha then departed into solitude. Bhante G explicitly distinguishes this background story, drawn from other sources, from the text he is reading.

The sutta’s narrative resumes with Ānanda explaining that the Buddha should not be followed when he leaves in this way. Later, monks who have not heard a teaching from him for some time ask to visit. Ānanda accompanies them to Pārileyyaka, where the Buddha teaches them. During the talk, one monk wonders what knowing and seeing would bring about the destruction of the taints. The Buddha recognizes the question and answers the gathered community.

The 37 aids to awakening

Before developing that answer, Bhante G reviews the seven groups that together comprise the 37 aids to awakening: the four establishments of mindfulness, four right efforts, four bases for spiritual power, five spiritual faculties, five powers, seven factors of enlightenment, and Noble Eightfold Path. Mindfulness concerns the body, feelings, mind, and mental objects. Right effort prevents unarisen unwholesome states, overcomes those already present, develops unarisen wholesome states, and maintains and strengthens wholesome states that have arisen. He explains that the spiritual faculties become powers through repeated cultivation. He also names the seven enlightenment factors—mindfulness, investigation, effort, joy, tranquility, concentration, and equanimity—and reviews the eightfold path.

How views of self arise

The central analysis begins with an ordinary person who regards form as self. Bhante G draws attention to the act of regarding: this view is itself a formation. The question then becomes where that formation comes from. In the discourse’s explanation, feeling associated with contact conditioned by ignorance gives rise to craving, and the formation arises from that craving. The inquiry therefore examines the conditions of a view, including the experience and craving that sustain it.

The same examination applies to other ways of making a self out of experience. A person may regard self as possessing form, form as contained within self, or self as contained within form. Bhante G connects these patterns with the twenty notions of self associated with the five aggregates: form, feeling, perception, volitional formations, and consciousness. The important point is that each act of identification is a formation whose arising can be investigated.

The analysis also reaches beyond these identifications. The view of an enduring, unchanging self is a formation. An annihilationist view is a formation as well. Even perplexity, doubt, and indecision concerning the true Dhamma are examined in the same way. Each can be traced to conditions rather than taken as an independent or permanent standpoint.

Seeing the conditions of a view

Bhante G repeatedly returns to the qualities of the formation and its conditions. The formation, craving, feeling, contact, and ignorance are all impermanent, conditioned, and dependently arisen. The discourse connects the destruction of the taints with knowing and seeing in this way. Its emphasis is on insight into the conditioned process through which views arise, not on the simple possession of a particular belief. The overview of the 37 aids provides the wider practice context for this examination. Bhante G closes the teaching by directing listeners back to the discourse and reiterating that his opening account of the quarrel was supplementary background.
