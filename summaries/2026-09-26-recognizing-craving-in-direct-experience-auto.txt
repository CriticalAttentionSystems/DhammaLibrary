From understanding the teaching to practicing it

Bhante G continues the concluding section of the mindfulness discourse by bringing the Four Noble Truths into direct experience. It is necessary to understand suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path. But a meditator must do more than repeat that craving is the cause of suffering. Notice craving when it arises, where it becomes established, and how it can be abandoned.

The place where craving appears is also the place for practice. If it arises in relation to a visible form, observe that experience. If it concerns a sound, a memory, a plan, or an opinion, attend there. Freedom is not found by looking for some unrelated place outside the experience in which the mind is caught.

The six sense doors

The field of observation includes eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind, together with their objects. Mental objects matter as much as sights and sounds: remembering, imagining, planning, and holding opinions can all provide occasions for craving.

The sense faculties and their objects are not, by themselves, the problem. The difficulty is the process that unfolds without mindfulness and clear comprehension. In the visual example, an object is seen, consciousness and contact occur, feeling arises, and perception recognizes the object. The mind may move toward what is pleasant or resist what is unpleasant. Thought then builds around the experience, and repeated examination continues to hold it in attention. A simple encounter can become an elaborate story of wanting.

Bhante G discusses ten aspects associated with each of the six sense doors, making sixty places where craving can arise and become established. The point is practical: use the framework to recognize where the mind is becoming entangled.

Recognize craving without condemning yourself

When craving appears, do not become ashamed, angry, or discouraged. Do not conceal it or blindly obey it. Know it as craving. Mindfulness remembers to observe; clear comprehension understands what is happening.

This changes the relationship to desire. Without awareness, craving becomes absorbed into a story about my wants, my feelings, my opinions, or my problems. With clearer observation, it can be recognized as a conditioned mental process. It arises when supporting conditions are present; it is not an independent, permanent self.

A pleasant memory can be repeatedly enjoyed and pursued. A painful memory can be resisted and rehearsed. Both are opportunities to see how the mind holds an experience and feeds its reaction. Knowing the process while it happens begins to interrupt its automatic momentum.

Quietness is not yet the end of craving

Bhante G repeatedly distinguishes recognizing craving, its becoming established around an object, and its complete abandonment. A desire may grow strong, weaken, or become quiet without its underlying tendency being uprooted. Under similar conditions it can return. Temporary peace should therefore not be mistaken for final cessation.

Wisdom examines the very experience to which craving attaches. See its impermanence, unsatisfactoriness, dependence on conditions, and lack of a permanent self. These are ways of looking, not words to repeat mechanically. As their meaning becomes clear in experience, fascination weakens, dispassion becomes possible, and the mind moves toward release.

The work begins wherever craving is actually operating. Understanding the origin of suffering in this immediate way prepares the meditator to understand how that same craving can cease.
