Returning to the five aggregates

Bhante G introduces the Full-Moon Night discourse by explaining why repeated attention to the five aggregates remains valuable. Hearing familiar teachings again can deepen understanding of one's own body and mind. Because suffering recurs, the subject deserves undivided, mindful attention rather than dismissal as something already learned.

The discourse takes place during a full-moon gathering of the Buddha and a community of monks. A monk asks permission to raise questions, and the Buddha invites him to sit down and ask. Bhante G comments that this arrangement allows the gathering to maintain its attention without repeatedly looking around or upward. The questions develop a sustained inquiry into form, feeling, perception, volitional formations, and consciousness.

Desire and clinging

The first questions establish the five aggregates subject to clinging and identify desire as their root. The next question distinguishes the aggregates from the clinging associated with them. Clinging is neither simply identical with the aggregates nor something apart from them: the desire and lust directed toward them is the clinging involved.

Such desire can take different forms. Someone may wish for a particular body, feeling, perception, formation, or consciousness in the future. Bhante G points out that wanting is not confined to one's present life. It can also reach toward the experiences one hopes to have later.

The term aggregate includes the full range of each category: past, present, and future; internal and external; gross and subtle; inferior and superior; far and near. The inquiry therefore extends beyond whatever is immediately apparent in the present moment.

Conditions for the aggregates

The monk next asks about the causes and conditions through which the aggregates become manifest. Bhante G draws attention to the different answers. Form depends on the four great elements. Contact is the cause and condition for feeling, perception, and volitional formations. Name-and-form is the cause and condition for consciousness.

He emphasizes that feeling arises only when there is contact. He also groups the answers so listeners can recognize their pattern: one aggregate is associated with the four elements, three with contact, and consciousness with name-and-form.

Twenty forms of identity view

Identity view arises when an uninstructed person relates to an aggregate in one of four ways: taking it to be self, taking self to possess it, placing the aggregate within self, or placing self within the aggregate. These four possibilities apply to each of the five aggregates, producing twenty forms of identity view.

Bhante G identifies this as belief in a permanent self and stresses how difficult it can be to overcome. The corresponding question asks how identity view does not arise. An instructed disciple does not regard form, feeling, perception, formations, or consciousness in any of these four ways. Understanding the teaching includes recognizing these patterns of identification.

Gratification, danger, and escape

Each aggregate is examined through three questions. Its gratification is the pleasure and joy that arise dependent on it. Its danger is that it is impermanent, bound up with suffering, and subject to change. Escape is the removal and abandonment of desire and lust for it.

Bhante G explains the pattern using form and consciousness, while making clear that it applies equally to feeling, perception, and formations. Recognizing what is attractive about experience belongs together with recognizing its changing nature and the possibility of releasing attachment.

Seeing without making a self

The discourse then asks how to know and see so that identification with I and mine, together with the underlying tendency to conceit, no longer occurs. The answer is to see every kind of aggregate with correct wisdom, without regarding it as one's possession, identity, or self. Bhante G highlights this as another important part of the inquiry.

Near the end, a monk's doubt concerns not-self and the effects of actions. Bhante G notes that people still raise this question. The Buddha responds by returning to the aggregates: are they permanent or impermanent, and is what changes and involves suffering suitable to regard as mine or self? The closing exchange brings the inquiry back to the changing nature of experience and the identification that the teaching asks practitioners to understand.
