Bhante G addresses eight questions ranging from everyday habits and family responsibilities to liberation and meditation. His answers emphasize practical adjustments, distinctions between different meanings of Buddhist terms, and attention to what practitioners can observe in their own experience.

Habitual viewing and family responsibilities

The first question concerns a decades-long attachment to television and YouTube. Bhante G recommends watching briefly when the content serves a useful purpose, then turning it off and returning to other activities. Reading and listening to Dhamma talks are among the alternatives he mentions. He illustrates the danger of unchecked viewing with a person who switched between two televisions so that watching could continue when one overheated.

The next question asks whether the instruction against keeping track of others’ omissions and failures also applies to adult children and family members. Bhante G distinguishes this from noticing repeated behavior that calls for a response within the family. Harmful actions should be discouraged, while wholesome actions should be encouraged. His explanation centers on the responsibility and influence a person may have within a family.

Dhamma and liberation

Asked whether Dhamma is impermanent, Bhante G distinguishes two uses of the word. Memories, ideas, and imaginings are mental objects that change. By contrast, he describes the truths taught by the Buddha as enduring: conditioned things are impermanent and unsatisfactory, and dependent origination remains true whether or not a Buddha appears. His claim concerns the continuing validity of these principles. He gives the discomfort accompanying anger as another example of a relationship that remains true.

A question about the knowledge and vision of liberation asks whether this attainment is limited to arahants. Bhante G answers affirmatively in the sense raised by the question: a fully liberated person knows that liberation has been attained, the holy life has been lived, and the necessary work has been completed. He contrasts this certainty with what an ordinary person can claim.

A question about resuscitation

Asked about refusing resuscitation or ventilator support for a severely disabled, bedridden parent, Bhante G treats the question cautiously. He warns that inability to speak or move does not establish that a person has no wish to live. He then gives his personal view of a different situation, in which the brain is completely dead. He says medical professionals should be consulted to establish the condition and suggests that, in that circumstance, refusing resuscitation may not be unethical. The answer is conditional, rather than a general judgment about elderly or disabled people.

Subtle breathing and direct observation

When a practitioner cannot feel the breath, Bhante G explains that breathing may have become very subtle. He recounts a farmer finding missing buffalo by going to the water where they are likely to be. Similarly, the meditator returns attention to the nostrils, where the breath’s touch is expected, to find the breath again. He also suggests taking quick breaths to make the sensation apparent again.

Another practitioner worries that contemplating impermanence, unsatisfactoriness, and selflessness may amount to self-suggestion. Bhante G directs attention to breathing, feelings, thoughts, and other immediate experiences. One breath ends and another follows; thoughts arise and pass away. He presents the repeated need to breathe and the limits of control as features to notice directly. The instruction is to give experience undivided attention without forcing a conclusion or manufacturing what one expects to find.

Recollection, loving-friendliness, and fetters

The final question concerns a practice of recitation and loving-friendliness that reduces agitation but does not sustain deep tranquility and equanimity. Bhante G encourages continuing. Recalling the Buddha’s qualities strengthens faith and confidence; loving-friendliness reduces anger and resentment. These developments support tranquility and equanimity. He also recommends recollecting the qualities of the Dhamma and Sangha.

He distinguishes gradual reduction from complete abandonment of fetters. Stream-entry removes three lower fetters, the second stage further weakens the remaining lower fetters, and the third stage eliminates the lower fetters completely. His practical response is to continue cultivating the qualities already described. After the closing recitations, he adds a brief encouragement to study Dhamma: greater understanding supports meditation, arouses spiritual urgency, and contributes to liberation from suffering.
