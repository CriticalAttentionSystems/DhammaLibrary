How can I work with aversion during meditation?

A participant describes physical and emotional tension while trying to develop access concentration and asks how to deal with what seems to be ill will. Bhante G recommends beginning every sitting with loving-friendliness, mettā. This can help prevent ill will or make it easier to recognize and address when it appears.

If ill will does arise, do not simply press on toward concentration. Use mindfulness to observe how it arises, how long it remains, and how it passes away. Bhante G brings the practice back to impermanence, unsatisfactoriness, and the absence of a permanent self in experience. The question asks for a book, but his response offers these practical instructions rather than a particular title.

Is caring for my health craving for existence?

The next question asks whether eating well, exercising, and wanting a long life amount to craving for existence. Bhante G explains the desire for sensual pleasures and the wish to continue enjoying them in future lives. Unfulfilled wanting carries forward the search for satisfaction; continuing to feed it also continues suffering.

He makes an important distinction: eating healthy food and exercising to maintain the body are good. Caring for health need not be abandoned. At the same time, work to reduce craving for pleasures and continued existence. The purpose of meditation is to end suffering, rather than keep extending the desires that sustain it.

Must I choose between honesty and kindness?

Bhante G advises practicing both. Honesty matters, but it can be expressed in gentle, polite, friendly words. Choose suitable words and circumstances so that being truthful does not become an excuse for hurting another person.

The moderator adds a recommendation to read MN 58, where the Buddha discusses speech in relation to truth and benefit. This is offered as further reading alongside Bhante G's practical advice to bring honesty and care together.

What do change, decay, dispassion, and cessation mean?

Asked about several related expressions, Bhante G approaches them as ways of examining impermanence. Things change, deteriorate, and disappear. Passion can also fade and cease. He illustrates the explanation with the sound of a gong: loud at first, it gradually fades until it is no longer heard. The faculty of sight likewise changes; its strength can diminish as a person grows older.

These experiences arise through causes and conditions. Seeing their dependence and change supports the understanding that they are not mine, not what I am, and not a permanent self. The point is to loosen identification with what cannot remain as we wish, rather than merely learn a set of definitions.

How does mindfulness of breathing reduce craving?

Bhante G places mindfulness of breathing within right mindfulness on the Noble Eightfold Path. As arising and passing become clearer, the mind sees the difficulty of holding on to an experience that is already disappearing. Understanding weakens the ignorance that supports craving.

He draws attention to the final four breathing instructions: contemplate impermanence, dispassion, cessation, and relinquishment while breathing in and out. Relinquishment means letting go without continuing to cling to what has left the mind.

He also distinguishes ordinary arising and passing from cessation without renewed arising, which he identifies as nibbāna. Noticing that a changing experience ends should therefore not be confused with the complete ending of suffering. The practice develops through seeing clearly and learning to release attachment.

What if a slow, long breath feels boring?

A participant finds it harder to stay attentive when breathing becomes slow and long. Bhante G encourages appreciation of the relaxation of body and mind. Tranquility supports concentration; this is an opportunity to stay with the breath rather than dismiss the experience as uninteresting.

He directs attention to the transition where exhalation ends and inhalation begins, observing breathing as a continuous process. His advice is to remain attentive to this calm experience. He does not instruct the participant to force the breath into a particular length or rhythm.

Can loving-friendliness encompass the five aggregates?

Bhante G answers yes, then emphasizes that loving-friendliness has no boundary. He points to the many beings behind ordinary experience. Food depends on people, animals, bees, and other conditions. A chair, building, or vehicle reflects the work of carpenters, builders, and many others. Even an inanimate object can remind us of living beings whose efforts helped bring it into our lives.

Let loving-friendliness reach beyond those immediately seen or known. Bhante G emphasizes including all beings, known or unknown, visible or invisible, without excluding anyone. Such an inclusive intention provides a strong beginning for meditation.
