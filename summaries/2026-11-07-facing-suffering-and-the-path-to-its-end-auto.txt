Facing suffering and the path to its end

A truth to face honestly

Bhante G presents the Four Noble Truths as a distinctive teaching of the Buddhas. He begins with the observation that truth can seem bitter to people who do not want to face it, while the Buddha describes truth as sweet. Suffering is an uncomfortable subject, yet understanding it is essential to the teaching he develops.

He describes a progression in the Buddha’s instruction: generosity, moral conduct, the pleasures of a favorable existence, and the dangers within pleasure. What is enjoyable changes. Recognizing that change prepares a person to examine suffering rather than expect lasting satisfaction from what cannot remain the same. Bhante G asks listeners to consider their own experience sincerely and acknowledge what they find.

Birth, growth, and the demands of life

The explanation of suffering begins with birth. Bhante G points to the mother’s labor and the newborn’s cry. He then extends the cry as a metaphor for the continuing demands of life: milk, food, clothing, medicine, education, shelter, and comfort. The audible crying may stop, but the need for something more continues inwardly. His invitation is to recognize that need through honest reflection on one’s own life.

Growth, maturity, and aging are examined as parts of the same process. Children may long to be adults so they can do what adults do. Later, the process called growing reveals itself through graying hair, weaker senses, difficulty walking, and other signs of age. Changing the name from growth to maturity does not stop the change itself.

Sickness and death are further aspects of the discussion. Having health insurance does not remove the pain of being ill. Bhante G describes bodily decay as a process already underway during life, culminating in death. These examples bring the teaching back to experiences that people can recognize for themselves.

What wanting cannot secure

Not getting what one wants brings suffering. Bhante G also asks listeners to examine what happens after a wish is fulfilled. An object may bring pleasure initially, but its satisfaction diminishes. Interest fades, and another object becomes attractive. He calls these the two tragedies of not getting what one wants and getting it without finding lasting satisfaction.

Wishing alone cannot prevent aging or death. He applies the same point to rebirth: in the account he gives, actions have already established conditions for further birth, and simply wishing for no more lives does not remove those conditions. The talk therefore moves beyond expressing a preference toward understanding what sustains suffering.

Bhante G summarizes this discussion through the five aggregates: form, feeling, perception, volitional formations, and consciousness. Each is impermanent. The examination of suffering includes these changing aspects of experience as well as the painful events of life.

Craving keeps asking for more

The second truth identifies the cause of suffering as craving, described through desire, lust, and thirst. Bhante G acknowledges the familiar objection that people need desires to work, establish a family, obtain housing, and meet other needs. He distinguishes desire that perpetuates wanting from the aspiration to become free of desire, which he associates with liberation.

To illustrate craving’s insatiability, he tells of a person whose house fills with a rain of gold. There is more than enough for the household and even the wider community. Yet the owner wishes the house had been larger so it could have held more. The example shows how receiving more does not necessarily bring wanting to an end.

Unfulfilled desire can continue throughout life. The point of investigating craving is to understand why seeking another satisfying object repeatedly leaves the underlying demand intact.

Cessation and the way to it

The third truth is that suffering can come to an end through liberation. The fourth identifies the way leading to that cessation: the Noble Eightfold Path. Bhante G lists right understanding, right thinking, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration. He urges practice of the path as the means of bringing suffering to an end.

He explains the four truths through an analogy with illness. There is an illness, it has a cause, it can be cured, and there is a way to that cure. These form a connected four-part account. The teaching joins the recognition of suffering to the possibility of release and the practice that supports it.

In brief closing advice after the meditation and recitations, Bhante G encourages asking questions as a way to strengthen investigation, a factor of awakening. He again encourages mindfulness practice and liberation from suffering.
