Understanding what is wholesome

Opening a retreat on the Noble Eightfold Path, Bhante G begins with right understanding, also called right view. He draws on a discourse that unfolds the subject through a series of questions and explanations. Its concern is practical liberation, not merely the ability to repeat a definition.

The first approach recognizes the unwholesome as unwholesome, its roots as its roots, the wholesome as wholesome, and its roots as its roots. Conduct must be understood in terms of the suffering it causes and the qualities of mind it expresses.

Actions of body, speech, and mind

Bhante G discusses ten unwholesome courses of action. Three concern bodily conduct: killing, stealing, and sensual misconduct. Four concern speech: lying, divisive speech, harsh speech, and gossip. Three concern mind: covetousness, ill will, and wrong view.

His explanations connect these actions to their consequences. Killing and stealing injure others and trouble the conscience. Lying undermines trust. Divisive speech breaks friendships, and harsh words wound people. Covetousness looks at another person's property with the wish to possess it; hatred harms the mind that carries it.

Greed, hatred, and delusion are the roots of the unwholesome. Their opposites—non-greed, non-hatred, and non-delusion—support wholesome conduct. Understanding these distinctions should lead to practice: refrain from the harmful actions and cultivate their wholesome alternatives.

Nutriment and dependence

A second approach considers the supports on which life and experience depend. Bhante G discusses material food together with contact, thought or intention, and consciousness. He invites listeners to understand these supports, their origin, their cessation, and the way leading to cessation.

The same pattern will recur throughout the talk. Instead of viewing an experience as an isolated possession, investigate what sustains it and how those conditions can change. The Noble Eightfold Path remains the training through which this understanding develops.

The Four Noble Truths

The most familiar explanation of right view is understanding suffering, its cause, its cessation, and the path. Birth, aging, death, separation from what is loved, association with what is disliked, and the aggregates affected by clinging are included in the discussion of suffering.

Craving is described as thirst: for sensual pleasure, for becoming, and for non-becoming. The end of suffering is the complete abandonment of craving, not merely its temporary interruption. The path is right understanding, right thought, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration.

Right view is therefore inseparable from the rest of the training. Understanding its purpose should affect how one thinks, speaks, acts, works, and practices meditation.

Following the conditions backward

Bhante G then uses dependent origination to explore further ways of understanding. Beginning near the end of the sequence—with aging, death, sorrow, and distress—he works backward through birth, becoming, clinging, craving, and their conditions. For each, the inquiry asks what it is, what gives rise to it, how it ceases, and what path leads to its cessation.

Clinging includes attachment to sensual pleasure, views, rules and rituals, and ideas of self. The talk repeatedly returns to the way the mind constructs and defends an “I.” Conventional language may use such expressions, but the investigation challenges the assumption of a permanent entity behind them.

The source discourse offers sixteen approaches to right understanding; this talk develops several and indicates the wider sequence. Its repeated conclusion is the same: understanding must help abandon the underlying tendencies to lust, hatred, conceit, and ignorance. The value of right view is realized when it contributes to freedom from suffering in this very life.
