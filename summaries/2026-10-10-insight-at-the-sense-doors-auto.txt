Investigating experience at the sense doors

Bhante G continues his explanation of the cessation of suffering through contemplation of the senses. Having previously discussed the eye and ear, he uses the nose as a worked example. The same pattern applies to the tongue, body, and mind, which he asks listeners to investigate for themselves. Repetition helps establish and remember the practice, but the purpose is to understand experience directly. The words are not a mantra to recite without observation.

Here, the nose means the faculty of smelling rather than the organ's outer appearance. A smell is known through nose consciousness; contact occurs when the smelling faculty, smell, and corresponding consciousness come together. Feeling arises dependent on that contact. Bhante G also considers perception of smell, craving, thoughts, and examination of the object.

At each point, the meditator observes impermanence, the inability to provide lasting satisfaction, and dependence on conditions. These experiences are subject to change, decay, and disappearance. They are not to be taken as a possession or identity: “This is not mine. This I am not. This is not myself.” Applying this understanding where desire arises is the practical work of meditation.

Preparing the mind to see clearly

Bhante G describes the learned noble disciple as a prepared practitioner. Faith in the Buddha, hearing and learning the Dhamma, wise reflection, moral conduct, regular meditation, mindfulness, and clear comprehension support the ability to perceive with wisdom.

Without this preparation, experiences such as feeling, craving, and thought are readily taken as “me” or “mine.” With it, the meditator can investigate them as conditioned phenomena. Wisdom is applied to the very place where craving arises. In his example of seeing, this includes the eye, visible form, consciousness, contact, feeling, and the mental responses that follow. The point is to see their actual nature, rather than merely think about a teaching.

Disenchantment and the fading of passion

Clear seeing changes the mind's relationship to an object. It no longer trusts that something impermanent and conditioned can provide lasting satisfaction. Bhante G calls this disenchantment: the object's power to deceive the mind is broken. It does not mean sadness, hatred, or depression. Craving begins to lose its foundation because the mind is no longer blindly fascinated.

Disenchantment supports dispassion, the fading of attachment. This is not achieved by forcibly suppressing passion. As the object is understood, the heat of attachment cools. Seeing, hearing, and thinking may continue, but the mind no longer runs toward their objects with the same grasping. The practice concerns release from that grasping, not the disappearance of sensory experience.

Knowing freedom through practice

Bhante G traces the sequence from perception with wisdom to disenchantment, dispassion, freedom, and direct knowledge of freedom. When craving and clinging no longer bind the mind to an object, that freedom can be known in experience. He distinguishes this from calmness that comes and goes: the question is whether the mind is bound by grasping.

The repeated formula also describes the completion of the path, when the causes of rebirth are exhausted and the training is fulfilled. These statements express the culmination of practice; repeating the formula alone does not establish that attainment. Bhante G emphasizes how much there is to understand and practice, and encourages listeners to continue diligently so that the teaching becomes clear in their own lives. He leaves the remaining explanation for a later session.
