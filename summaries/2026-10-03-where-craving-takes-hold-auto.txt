Where craving takes hold and how it ceases

Find craving in present experience

Continuing his teaching on the Four Noble Truths in the mindfulness discourse, Bhante G asks where craving arises and where it becomes established. These questions are meant to direct attention to experience. It is not enough to know the statement that craving causes suffering; notice the process while it is happening.

Craving appears through the six sense doors: seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting, touching, and thinking. Something seems pleasant or agreeable, and the mind begins to want it. Memories, imagination, opinions, and plans belong to this field of observation just as much as visible objects and sounds.

A practical map of experience

Bhante G describes ten aspects associated with each sense door: the faculty, its object, consciousness, contact, feeling born of contact, perception, volition, craving, thought, and examination. Applied across the six doors, these make sixty places where craving can arise and take hold. They are aspects of experience to investigate, rather than sixty different kinds of attractive objects.

In the visual example, a form is seen, eye-consciousness and contact occur, and feeling arises. Perception recognizes the object, volition moves toward it, and craving appears. Thought and examination can continue holding that object in the mind. The meditator learns to recognize the process at the place where it is occurring.

A memory offers another example. The mind may enjoy a pleasant memory, repeat it, and want the experience again. It may resist a painful memory. Either can become an occasion for craving through the mind door. Looking only at external objects would miss this part of the practice.

Observe without blame or concealment

When craving appears, do not become guilty, ashamed, angry, or discouraged. Do not hide it or simply follow it. Mindfulness remembers to observe, while clear comprehension understands what is happening. Be precise: know the wanting connected with this sight, this sound, this feeling, or this thought.

With weak mindfulness, craving quickly becomes my desire, my memory, my opinion, or my problem. Clear observation reveals a mental process that depends on conditions. Seeing it in this way weakens its power to control the mind. The very place where craving arises is the place to understand its support and its abandonment.

Becoming quiet is not the same as ceasing

Bhante G pauses over the expression that craving settles down. Here it means that craving has found a footing in relation to an object. It does not mean that craving has been fully abandoned.

A desire may become strong, weaken, and grow quiet while its underlying tendency remains. When similar conditions return, it can arise again. Temporary quietness therefore must not be mistaken for complete cessation. Understanding where craving arises and how it is sustained prepares the meditator to understand where it must be relinquished.

Contemplating the eye and ear

The talk then turns to the truth of cessation. Bhante G works through the eye and ear in detail, applying the same contemplation to each of the ten aspects. The sense faculties, their objects, consciousness, contact, feelings, perceptions, volitions, craving, thoughts, and examination are impermanent, unsatisfactory, subject to decay and cessation, and dependent on conditions. None can be held as a permanent possession or self.

Seeing this with wisdom leads toward disenchantment, the fading of passion, freedom, and knowledge of that freedom. The repeated passage culminates in the completion of the holy life and the exhaustion of rebirth. This is the liberating outcome toward which the practice is directed, rather than a claim that a moment of quiet has already completed the work.

Bhante G repeats the contemplation so that listeners will practice each part instead of assuming they already understand it. The detailed treatment here covers only the eye and ear. The nose, tongue, body, and mind follow the same pattern and are left for the continuation of the teaching.
