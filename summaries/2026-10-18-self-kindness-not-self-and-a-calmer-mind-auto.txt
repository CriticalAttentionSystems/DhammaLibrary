Breathing and loving-friendliness for sleeplessness

A question carried over from the previous week asks about difficulty sleeping because of anxiety or overthinking. Drawing on his own experience, Bhante G suggests attending to breathing or practicing mettā, loving-friendliness, to relax the mind and body. Attention can rest on inhaling and exhaling at the nostrils or on the abdomen’s rising and falling. He advises attending directly to these sensations without verbalizing or conceptualizing, and presents these practices as approaches that may help the mind settle and sleep come.

Loosening identification with “I” and “mine”

Asked how to reduce the conceit of “mine, I, myself,” he recommends observing experience as conditioned, dependently arisen, impermanent, and passing away, both during meditation and outside it. Such observation supports the recognition that these changing phenomena are not mine, not what I am, and not myself. He emphasizes that identification weakens gradually through practice.

He describes four ways of identifying with each of the five aggregates: regarding an aggregate as self, regarding self as possessing it, placing the aggregate within self, or placing self within the aggregate. Applied to form, feeling, perception, thought, and consciousness, these make twenty forms of self-identification. Seeing their changing and conditioned nature is presented as a practical way to loosen these views.

Memory, craving, and insight

A participant asks whether recurring memories of stressful events, including those associated with PTSD, are craving or clinging. Bhante G’s answer makes a general distinction: having a memory does not itself require either craving or clinging. He gives examples of remembering a conversation, recalling help given to others, and recognizing that anger, greed, or confusion have diminished through meditation. He connects mindfulness with remembering and noticing one’s progress.

The second part of the question asks about using insight meditation to observe the effects of a memory and approach that experience through impermanence and not-self. He affirms this approach. His response concerns how to contemplate remembered experience; it does not identify every recurring memory as an expression of attachment.

Disenchantment, dispassion, and examination

Disenchantment is explained as an opening of wisdom through seeing experience clearly. Observing things change can weaken craving, so disenchantment is not presented as a negative attitude toward life. Bhante G distinguishes it from dispassion by describing a causal relationship: disenchantment leads to dispassion, the fading of passion or craving.

A further question asks what “examination” means in meditation. He describes investigating a mental process: how a state arose, what caused it, and how it led to the present experience. Craving and subsequent thought provide an example. He connects this inquiry with investigation as a factor of awakening. Mindfulness itself can be investigated by considering how it developed, what supported it, and how it became more complete.

Self-kindness, emotions, and concentration

A participant describes difficulty recognizing emotions and feeling that repeated mettā phrases do not reach deeply enough. Bhante G begins with relaxation and a less punitive attitude toward oneself. Recognizing that nobody is perfect, that everyone has weaknesses, and that mistakes happen can help soften rigidity and self-criticism. He encourages noticing emotions and considering their causes, including past experience, while learning to release continual recollection of the past and return to the present.

Mettā is more than mechanical repetition, but he also stresses that repeated practice can have an effect without producing immediate results. Even a short set of wishes for well-being, peace, patience, courage, and understanding can support a calmer mind. He links calm with concentration and agrees that clearer awareness can help practitioners recognize emotions and develop appropriate self-love. This kindness is distinguished from selfish attachment.

For aversion and painful tension during attempts at concentration, his advice remains consistent: relax, cultivate peace, and avoid forcing the mind. Excessive effort can bring contraction, anxiety, and worry. He recommends loving-friendliness toward oneself and allowing concentration to develop with a relaxed state of mind.

Remembering and applying the teachings

Asked for a chant or discourse supporting awareness of impermanence and not-self, Bhante G recommends the Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta and the Girimānanda Sutta. After a loving-friendliness recitation, he adds a substantive explanation: its wording is systematic and meaningful, and remembering it can support mettā toward all living beings and then toward oneself. He connects this practice with the earlier questions about aversion and sleeplessness, encouraging practitioners to observe its benefits in their own experience.
