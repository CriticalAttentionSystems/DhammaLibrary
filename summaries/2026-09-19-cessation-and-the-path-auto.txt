Why suffering can end

After discussing suffering and its origin in earlier talks, Bhante G turns to the Third Noble Truth. Suffering is conditioned: it depends on causes. When craving is abandoned, clinging loses its support; when the supporting conditions cease, the process of suffering can cease.

He describes nibbāna as the extinguishing of greed, hatred, and delusion. This does not mean destroying a person or entering blank nothingness. The teaching concerns the ending of the conditions that bind the mind to suffering. Nibbāna is described as peace, freedom, and the unconditioned.

Everyday experience makes part of the principle understandable. When anger subsides, there is relief. When insistent desire fades, the mind becomes more peaceful. Yet temporary relief is different from liberation: a particular disturbance can quiet down while its roots remain. The complete ending of suffering requires the uprooting of those roots.

Wisdom weakens craving

Craving appears repeatedly through sights, sounds, smells, tastes, bodily sensations, and mental activity. Wherever it occurs, it can be known. Seeing impermanence weakens attachment to what cannot stay; seeing unsatisfactoriness weakens blind desire; seeing the absence of a permanent self weakens possessiveness and conceit.

The Third Noble Truth therefore offers hope without making practice sound effortless. The mind can be trained. A passing taste of peace points toward the possibility of complete freedom, but it must be understood together with suffering and its cause.

The Noble Eightfold Path

The Fourth Noble Truth supplies the training, rather than leaving liberation as a distant promise. Bhante G describes its eight connected factors:

- Right understanding recognizes the Four Noble Truths and sees experience clearly.
- Right thought turns toward renunciation, non-ill will, and harmlessness.
- Right speech abstains from false, divisive, harsh, and useless speech.
- Right action abstains from killing, stealing, and sexual misconduct.
- Right livelihood earns a living without harming or deceiving oneself or others.
- Right effort prevents and abandons unwholesome states while developing and maintaining wholesome ones.
- Right mindfulness observes body, feeling, mind, and phenomena with clear awareness.
- Right concentration gathers the mind into a calm, stable, unified state that supports insight.

These are parts of one path. They also form three mutually supporting trainings: morality, concentration, and wisdom. Harmless conduct gives the mind a cleaner foundation; concentration steadies it; wisdom understands experience as it is.

A middle way that must be practiced

The path avoids both devotion to sensual pleasure and the tormenting of the body. Neither indulgence nor self-mortification removes ignorance and craving. The middle way trains conduct and mind so that understanding can mature.

Bhante G compares the Four Noble Truths to illness, its cause, a cure, and the treatment. Hearing the name of a medicine does not heal a patient. Likewise, reciting the eight factors does not replace practicing honest speech, restraint, wise effort, mindfulness, and concentration.

One complete investigation

The four truths belong together. Naming suffering without showing its ending would discourage people. Naming its cause without a way to abandon it would leave them helpless. Declaring freedom without a path would give no practical direction.

Their pattern can be investigated in ordinary experience. When anger brings distress, ask what is being demanded or defended: respect, control, victory, or an image of oneself. See the conditions supporting the reaction and the effect of releasing them. The same examination applies to fear, jealousy, disappointment, and desire.

The work is to understand suffering fully, abandon its origin, realize its cessation, and develop the path. The Buddha shows the way, but each practitioner must walk it.
