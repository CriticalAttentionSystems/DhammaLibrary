Hearing and thought in jhāna

Asked whether all the senses must withdraw before a state counts as jhāna, Bhante G distinguishes the first absorption from the second. In his explanation, sound may still be heard in the first jhāna without receiving attention; hearing is suspended from the second onward. One need not wait for an immaterial attainment. Why feeling comes before perception

A participant asks how an experience can be felt as pleasant or unpleasant before it has been recognized. Bhante G describes a very rapid sequence involving the sense faculty, its object, consciousness, contact, feeling, and recognition. The events occur so quickly that an ordinary observer has difficulty distinguishing them. He cautions against assuming that our usual experience reveals every separate moment of the process. Loving-friendliness as an object of concentration

Unlike the sensation of breathing at the nostrils, loving-friendliness has no bodily boundary. Bhante G treats that boundlessness as part of the practice: attend to the wholesome state in which the mind is free of hatred, rather than searching for a sharply outlined physical sensation. The gathering of wholesome qualities can unify the mind and support concentration.

He adds an essential qualification. Loving-friendliness alone is not complete liberation. A state produced by the mind is conditioned and subject to change. Use the concentration it supports to investigate impermanence, unsatisfactoriness, and the absence of a permanent self. Concentration becomes a support for insight.

Working with a wandering mind

Return to the primary object, the breath, whenever thoughts carry attention away. For persistent wandering, Bhante G recommends counting a complete inhalation and exhalation as one. Count from one to ten and back down to one; then from one to nine and back; continue with successively shorter ranges until reaching one. If attention wanders and you cannot remember whether you were counting upward or downward, begin again.

A participant notices that counting can make the breath deliberate instead of natural. Bhante G acknowledges this directly: the exercise is a deliberate device for settling attention. His answer does not present counted breathing as an untouched observation of the natural breath.

Sublime dwelling and unwholesome memories

In explaining loving-friendliness as a divine or sublime dwelling, Bhante G describes a high quality of life that can be cultivated here, through wholesome states of mind. He relates the expression to the four sublime states.

Another participant asks about memories that stir unwholesome desires. Bhante G first points to the suffering in greed, hatred, and delusion: wanting leaves us dissatisfied, anger disturbs us, and confusion obscures what is happening. Recognizing those consequences helps us turn toward what is wholesome. He recommends recollecting the purity of the Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha, and moral conduct, and returning to the teaching. This is gradual training, not an instant removal of every unwanted thought.

Becoming and practice in daily life

To explain becoming, Bhante G uses familiar examples: through learning and activity, someone becomes a doctor, engineer, or professor. He then connects action with its results. The everyday examples help introduce a difficult term without making the whole explanation depend on abstract vocabulary.

For a busy householder, concentration requires priorities and some time apart from obligations. Bhante G recommends sitting quietly in the morning, attending to the breath without rehearsing what happened before or planning what comes next. Making this regular creates interest in returning to practice.

During the working day, stop for one or two minutes each hour and attend to the breath. Eight two-minute pauses amount to sixteen minutes of practice. These short periods help build continuity, enthusiasm, and familiarity. Concentration develops through repeated practice amid the responsibilities that both householders and monastics have.
