From calmness to knowing freedom

Bhante G concludes the final section of his teaching on the Four Foundations of Mindfulness. He begins with the difference between ordinary calm and liberation. Calmness can come and go. The freedom described here is release from craving and clinging, directly experienced by the mind.

Awareness of freedom and knowledge of freedom are related but distinguishable. The mind experiences release; wisdom understands that release has occurred and why. Knowing the causes of freedom helps the meditator avoid mistaking a peaceful state for the completion of the path.

Bhante G traces renewed existence to ignorance, craving, clinging, and becoming. When these causes are exhausted, the disciple knows that rebirth is exhausted. The stilling of formations that produce future becoming does not mean that the living body and mind immediately stop functioning.

What it means to complete the practice

Moral conduct, concentration, study, and other supports serve the purpose of liberation. Saying that the holy life has been lived means that this purpose has been fulfilled. The work described by the Four Noble Truths has been completed: suffering has been understood, its origin abandoned, cessation realized, and the path developed.

Bhante G presents a sequence of practice and result. Faith, learning the Dhamma, meditation, mindfulness, and clear comprehension prepare the disciple to perceive with wisdom. Clear seeing leads to disenchantment and dispassion; the fading of passion allows release and knowledge of release. These expressions are meant to describe what practice brings about, rather than remain ideas learned from a text.

Find craving where it is happening

Begin by establishing enough mindfulness and calm to observe experience. When craving arises, recognize it instead of immediately following or suppressing it. First identify the sense door: eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, or mind. Then investigate more precisely where the wanting finds support.

Bhante G reviews ten aspects at each of the six doors, making sixty places to investigate: the sense faculty, object, consciousness, contact, feeling, perception, volition, craving, thought, and examination. A desire connected with seeing might depend on an attractive form, the feeling born of contact, the perception of that form, or thoughts that keep it present. The point is to recognize the process in one's own experience.

If the exact connection is not yet clear, begin with the broader recognition that craving is arising through this sense door. Greater precision can develop as mindfulness becomes clearer. Where craving arises and takes hold is also where its abandonment must be practiced.

Use words to support clear observation

After recognizing the object of craving, apply the contemplation slowly and carefully. Observe it as impermanent, unable to provide lasting satisfaction, subject to decay and disappearance, conditioned, and dependent on causes. See that it cannot be possessed as a permanent self.

The words have practical functions. Impermanence directs attention to arising and passing. Decay and disappearance reveal that the object cannot remain as it is. Recognizing conditions loosens the assumption that experience stands independently or belongs to a lasting owner. The purpose is to see correctly, so that craving loses support, rather than fight craving or repeat words mechanically.

Practice patiently, again and again

Bhante G describes two uses of the contemplation. Apply it to a particular craving when it is recognized, and also rehearse the full sequence through the six sense doors as regular practice. Learning the sequence by heart helps the mind recognize what is happening before desire becomes strong.

At first, craving may be noticed only after it has gathered strength. With practice, it can be recognized earlier, when abandoning it is easier. Do not become discouraged because it returns repeatedly: each arising offers another opportunity to develop mindfulness and wisdom.

The object itself is not the problem; craving and clinging are. A desire becoming quiet for a while is not proof that its underlying tendency has ended. Practice moves from knowing craving to understanding and abandoning it. Bhante G closes by urging listeners to use this understanding in meditation, rather than leave it as something merely read or remembered.
