Concentration, impermanence, and not-self

Asked whether developing the divine eye is part of attaining the fourth jhana, Bhante G describes the fourth jhana as a necessary basis for developing this ability. His answer does not establish that the ability arises automatically with that attainment. A question about insight into arising and passing away and insight into cessation leads to an explanation of three discernible moments: arising, reaching a peak, and passing away. As mindfulness becomes clearer, sharper, and deeper, these phases can be observed more distinctly, even within a single sitting.

In response to a question about younger people who have no interest in Buddhism or meditation, he discusses how people's experiences shape their willingness to practice. Pain, frustration, or unhelpful meditation instructions can discourage people, while beneficial experience can sustain interest despite discomfort. He also observes that interest in meditation is growing. Asked how memories can exist without a self, he explains that remembering depends on changing conditions rather than a permanent inner entity. Memories are learned, reinforced through repetition, and sometimes forgotten. Their changing nature does not require an unchanging owner behind them.

Working with mindfulness of breathing

A question about moving through its four groups of exercises is answered in terms of mindfulness of body, feelings, mind, and mental objects. Experience gained with one area supports the next: training the mind to observe the body helps it recognize feelings, states of mind, and other phenomena. Bhante G also says that deeply practicing even one of these areas can support insight.

A participant asks about observing the body's “compression and decompression” while attending to breathing at the nose. Bhante G prefers the words “expansion and contraction.” Breathing involves movements in the abdomen and chest as well as sensations at the nostrils. Attention may move to a stronger sensation, such as the abdomen's rising and falling; he says there is no problem with noticing these movements while following the breath.

Recognizing progress

Seeing a bright light during meditation is not necessary for progress. Some meditators experience it and others do not, so Bhante G advises against trying to force the experience. In a follow-up answer, he points instead to observable changes in conduct and the mind: improved concentration, less anger, greater patience, more compassion, and a willingness to let others finish speaking. Practitioners can recognize the effects of practice by observing their own thoughts and reactions over time.

For self-criticism and high expectations, he advises attending to one's actual experience rather than judging oneself against other people or demanding that others fulfill personal expectations. A useful comparison recognizes shared conditions: feelings arise through contact, experiences change, and everyone is subject to aging and illness. Such observation differs from comparisons that feed jealousy, greed, or hatred.

Generosity, persistence, and emotional difficulty

Giving is described as a support for meditation because it encourages happiness, ease, compassion, and understanding. Holding tightly to possessions can bring fear of loss; sharing loosens that attachment and helps the mind become more comfortable with practice.

A participant who has practiced intermittently for forty years asks whether sincere effort still helps when concentration is difficult. Bhante G affirms the value of continued effort and recommends observing arising and passing away even without strong concentration. He encourages consistent daily practice, including morning and evening sessions, so meditation becomes a settled habit.

Finally, a question about profound darkness initially receives an answer about the ordinary darkness of closed eyes. When the moderator suggests that the question may refer to sadness or negative emotion, Bhante G recommends deliberately cultivating loving-friendliness toward all beings, using wishes for their well-being, happiness, and peace. A subsequent question about insomnia, anxiety, and overthinking is raised but deferred until another session.
